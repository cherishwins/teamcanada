# Fit For Gov — Narrative System

This is the strategic-narrative layer of the Fit For Gov brand system. It composes with two other skills. The `fitforgov-brand-kit` skill (v2.0) governs what a Fit For Gov surface *looks and sounds like* — palette, typography, seal usage, voice, banned words, post-type templates. The `fitforgov-persuasion` skill (v1.0) governs *how the words move the reader* — loss framing, anchoring, specificity, peer-incident proof, cost inversion, commitment pathway. This skill governs *what the practice is claiming to be and what story it is inside* — the positioning, the promise, the signature phrases, the active narrative strands, and the canonical facts they draw from.

When Claude is writing any Fit For Gov content, all three skills should be consulted together. The brand kit tells Claude how a sentence should sound. The persuasion skill tells Claude what shape that sentence should take to move a CAO. This skill tells Claude what the sentence is in fact claiming on behalf of the practice.

**Read this file fully before writing Fit For Gov content.** For the detailed narrative strands (water threat, SecTor validation, Hamilton prior-knowledge) read `references/narrative-strands.md`. For the source-cited facts library read `references/canonical-facts.md`. For two complete worked posts showing the doctrine deployed end-to-end read `references/worked-examples.md`.

## Part 1 — The Positioning Ladder

Fit For Gov sits on a three-rung ladder. Each rung has its own job, and copy uses them at different altitudes depending on the reader's starting context.

The top rung is *a civic-technology practice.* This is the brand identity — the line the seal itself carries, and the register in which Fit For Gov belongs in a reader's mind. It signals editorial seriousness, principal-led service, and a specific cultural positioning (civic, not startup; practice, not agency; technology, not services). Use this rung in the masthead, the LinkedIn About, the RFP cover page, and anywhere the brand is being introduced for the first time.

The middle rung is *custom software for Canadian municipalities.* This is the capability claim, the line that explains what a civic-technology practice actually produces. It matters because "civic technology" alone is abstract — a reader hearing "civic-technology practice" may not know whether Fit For Gov consults, researches, designs, develops, operates, or all of the above. "Custom software" is the concrete answer: this practice produces running code that municipalities operate in production. Use this rung in the second sentence of introductions, in capability statements on proposals, and in any explanation of what Fit For Gov will deliver.

The bottom rung is *municipal websites as the starting engagement.* This is the service offering currently filling the pipeline. It matters because it is the specific thing the practice is being hired to build today. Use this rung in specific engagement discussions and in the "what are we actually buying" answer.

The three rungs compose into the full introduction: *"Fit For Gov is a civic-technology practice that delivers custom software for Canadian municipalities, beginning with secure websites as the standard starting engagement and extending to whatever civic software the municipality subsequently needs built."* This is the complete introduction when a reader needs the complete introduction. In most copy, only one or two rungs appear, selected for the altitude of the conversation.

The rule for which rung to lead with is simple. If the reader is a stranger encountering the brand for the first time, lead with the top rung. If the reader is a CAO evaluating capability against a specific need, lead with the middle rung. If the reader is already in a procurement conversation and wants to know the specific thing being purchased, lead with the bottom rung. Never lead with "we build websites" in a first encounter — that is the bottom rung and it commodifies the practice before the reader has formed an impression of what it actually is.

## Part 2 — The Fit or Forward Promise

### The Commitment

The Fit or Forward promise is the practice's public brand commitment. In its canonical full form:

> **Fit or Forward.** If Fit For Gov is not the right team for the outcome your municipality requires, I will tell you that on the first call and connect you with the team that is. The answer is always yes or forward. It is never yes-when-no.

This commitment is binding on the principal personally. Every first call with a prospective municipality includes an explicit assessment of whether Fit For Gov is the right fit for what the municipality actually needs. If the assessment comes back negative, the call ends with a referral to the appropriate alternative practice, firm, or internal capability. The referral is specific — named person at named firm — not a vague "you might try looking at other vendors."

### Why It Works

The promise does four things at once. First, it inverts the default sales dynamic: a CAO expects a vendor call to be a pitch, and instead gets what reads as an advisory conversation. Second, it reduces the CAO's perceived risk of the call — they have nothing to defend against, because the vendor has already signaled they will recuse themselves if the fit is wrong. Third, it creates a positive-sum network effect: the firms Jesse refers to build goodwill toward Fit For Gov, and some of them reciprocate when they encounter engagements that aren't their fit but are Jesse's. Fourth, and most importantly, it is true — it describes a commitment the principal actually intends to honour, which means it strengthens rather than erodes over time as the practice scales.

### Usage Rules

The promise appears in three kinds of surfaces. First, it appears in the LinkedIn About section as a standalone paragraph. Second, it appears on the website, in a dedicated brand-promise block typically placed above the contact section. Third, it appears in the opening of the first call with any prospective municipality, as an explicit verbal commitment from the principal.

The promise does not appear in every LinkedIn post — overexposure would dilute it. It does appear in any post that introduces the practice to a new audience (launch posts, follower-milestone posts, year-end summaries) and in any post whose body has built a strong loss-framed case, where the promise serves as the trust-generating close. The test: would the reader, arriving at the promise, experience it as a natural extension of the evidence they've just been given? If yes, deploy. If the promise would feel bolted-on, omit and use the standard phone closer instead.

The operational rule that makes the promise credible is that the principal must actually execute the referral when the fit is wrong. Honesty at the sales stage is the single highest-ROI behaviour a founder-led practice can adopt.

### The Phrasing Family

"Fit or Forward" is the canonical two-word reference. When more space is available, the fuller phrasings, in increasing length:

| Form | Use case |
|------|----------|
| *Fit or Forward.* | Signature reference; end of a post body |
| *Fit or Forward — we tell you which on the first call.* | LinkedIn post closer, above the phone line |
| *If we aren't the fit, we forward you to who is.* | Conversational register; podcast interviews; emails |
| *The answer is always yes or forward. It is never yes-when-no.* | Full formal statement; LinkedIn About; brand-promise block on the site |

Treat these as a single phrase family and choose by available length. Do not invent alternative phrasings. The compactness is the point.

## Part 3 — The Signature Phrase Set

These are the narrative trademarks — the short, memorable phrases that carry the Fit For Gov brand across surfaces. Consistent repetition over twelve months is how a brand becomes recognizable on sight in a crowded feed. The set below is the formalized inventory. New phrases may be added only with the principal's explicit approval and with a clear function that existing phrases do not already cover.

### The Canonical Set

| Phrase | Function | Typical placement |
|--------|----------|-------------------|
| Call first. Email second. Forms third. | The service model — phone-first commitment | Homepage, email signature, LinkedIn About, every closer |
| Fit or Forward. | The referral promise | LinkedIn About, brand promise block, first-call opening |
| Under the threshold, over the standard. | Procurement plus quality | Procurement posts, RFP responses, capability statements |
| Municipal technology, principal-led. | The positioning line in seven words | Banners, proposal intros, OG images |
| The attack surface is a git repository. | Technical signature | Security posts, threat-brief Dossiers, ledger comparisons |
| The website is the reconnaissance surface. | The strategic hinge for critical-infrastructure stories | Water-threat posts, ICS-compromise posts, state-actor narratives |
| Read the briefing. | The Dossier CTA | Homepage masthead, Dossier release posts, email links |
| The principal signs every deliverable. | Founder-led governance signal | Proposals, RFP responses, pitch decks |
| Canadian-hosted, Canadian-run. | Data residency and sovereignty | Specifications, procurement notes, capability statements |
| Dossier, not deck. | Editorial voice over SaaS register | Pitch materials, proposal cover letters |
| The phone is the interface. | UX philosophy as brand statement | Service model descriptions, About pages |
| We build under the threshold. The threat doesn't care about the threshold. | Paired construction pairing procurement with risk | Technical-lead-focused posts |

### Usage Rules

Every Fit For Gov post carries at least one signature phrase and at most three. A post with zero signature phrases is a post that could have been written for any civic-tech practice, which is not what the brand is. A post with four or more signature phrases feels like a greatest-hits reel rather than a considered argument.

The phrases are interchangeable in register — any of them can appear in any post that fits the function — but they are not interchangeable in content. "The attack surface is a git repository" belongs in a post where the attack-surface concept is load-bearing. It does not belong in a procurement-only post. Match the phrase to the function it performs.

### What Is Not a Signature Phrase

The Fit For Gov brand does not operate with tagline-style slogans ("Secure by design," "Built for tomorrow"), promotional adjectives, or motivational closers. A phrase earns signature status by being specific, structural, and repeatable. "Built for tomorrow" is none of those. "The principal signs every deliverable" is all three.

## Part 4 — The Govtech Network Frame

The practice is well-connected in the Canadian civic-technology community through the principal's prior work on Waterworth, Muniworth, and The National Strategy Council, and through years of relationships built in the BC and Ontario municipal-tech spaces. This connectivity is a genuine asset, but it must be signalled without directly bragging, which would read as status-claiming and undermine the brand's editorial voice.

The solution is to let network depth manifest through *operational claims that only a connected person could credibly make*, rather than through roster-naming. The three canonical forms:

**The referral-speed claim.** *"In the Canadian civic-tech community, I can usually reach the right person within a week."* This sentence makes a specific, testable claim about what the network enables. It is not "I know everyone" — it is "the network is dense enough that I can find the right party quickly."

**The named-adjacency claim.** *"If your municipality needs something that isn't in my lane, I know the three or four Canadian practices that might be and I know the principals personally."* This is the Fit or Forward promise restated as a network fact. It acknowledges a defined but non-exhaustive set of alternatives and signals personal relationships with the principals running them.

**The prior-work claim.** *"Before launching Fit For Gov, I built the production front-ends for Waterworth, Muniworth, and The National Strategy Council."* This is already in the brand kit. The network implication is carried by the products named — recognizable Canadian municipal-tech platforms — and the reader who recognizes them infers the network depth without being told.

### Rules for Network Talk

The three claims above are the only network signals that appear in public copy. Specific named references beyond the prior-work roster — names of current colleagues, named mentors, friendships with specific CAOs — are private network information and do not appear on public surfaces. Name-dropping is strictly prohibited. When a particular name is relevant in a private sales conversation, it is referenced verbally on the call, never published.

The Fit or Forward promise is the governing frame for network talk on public surfaces. It turns the network from a bragging asset into an operational commitment to the prospective client.

## Part 5 — Active Narrative Strands

At any given time, Fit For Gov operates with two or three "active narrative strands" — strategic storylines that run across multiple posts and Dossiers over a period of weeks or months, giving coherence to individual pieces of content by placing them in a larger argumentative arc. The strands are not themes in the conventional marketing sense; they are specific, dated, evidence-grounded narratives with their own hinges and their own rules for how the Fit For Gov practice is positioned relative to them.

As of April 2026, the three active strands are: the Water-Threat Strand (centred on CCCS threat assessments to Canadian water infrastructure), the SecTor-Validation Strand (centred on the November 18, 2025 Mathieu and Roy study of Canadian government websites), and the Hamilton-Prior-Knowledge Strand (centred on the audit record showing Hamilton was aware of critical IT weaknesses three years before the 2024 ransomware attack). Each of these strands has its own hinge — the specific argumentative move that lets a websites practice speak about a threat landscape that extends beyond websites — and its own constraints on what can and cannot be claimed.

**For the full treatment of each strand, including the hinge, the phrasing family, the do-not rules, and the list of sources, read `references/narrative-strands.md`.**

The rule for using strands: every Fit For Gov post locates itself within at least one active strand. A post that is not inside a strand is either launching a new strand (which requires the principal's explicit decision) or is a standalone that should be flagged for review. Most posts sit inside one strand and optionally touch a second strand in their body — the strand is the tree, the post is the leaf, the month's content calendar is the branch pattern.

## Part 6 — The Canonical Facts Library

Every Fit For Gov claim traces to a specific public source. The canonical facts library is the inventory of those sources, organized by the claim they support. It is a running document that grows as new citable facts enter the public record and as stale facts are retired.

The current library covers four categories: named municipal incidents (Hamilton, Yellowknife), named supply-chain compromises (Essential Plugin, Smart Slider 3 Pro, Crisis24/OnSolve CodeRED), institutional assessments and studies (CCCS National Cyber Threat Assessment, CCCS water-infrastructure assessment, Auditor General findings, SecTor study), and procurement-mechanic facts (direct-award thresholds by province).

**For the full sourced inventory, read `references/canonical-facts.md`.**

The rule for using facts: every claim in a Fit For Gov post must be traceable to a fact in the library or to an equivalently-sourced external reference. If Claude is writing a post and reaches for a figure not in the library, Claude must either add it to the library (with source) or omit the claim. The library is the backstop that keeps the brand credible at the granular level.

## Part 7 — Integration Rules

### Composition With the Other Two Skills

This skill composes with `fitforgov-brand-kit` (visual and voice) and `fitforgov-persuasion` (reader psychology). When writing Fit For Gov content, Claude consults all three. The brand kit answers "what does this look and sound like." The persuasion skill answers "how does this move the reader." This Narrative System answers "what is the practice claiming to be and what story is it inside."

The three are redundant where they agree (the phone number, for example, is specified in all three) and complementary where they cover different ground. No conflict exists between them. Where a writer finds one, the writer escalates to the principal rather than resolving the conflict in copy.

### Order of Consultation

In practice, Claude should consult the three skills in the following order when producing new content. First, consult this skill to identify the active narrative strand the piece will sit within, the positioning rung that frames the opening, and the signature phrase(s) to deploy. Second, consult the brand kit to select the post type, the structural template, and the voice register. Third, consult the persuasion skill to tune the grammatical construction of the sentences themselves. The order matters because narrative decisions precede structural decisions precede grammatical decisions — inverting the order produces well-formed copy that is strategically aimless.

### Update Cadence

The narrative strands and the canonical facts library both update as the public record changes. The positioning ladder, the Fit or Forward promise, the signature phrase set, and the network frame are stable and rarely change — changes to any of these four require the principal's explicit decision and typically reflect a major repositioning rather than a news cycle.

### What To Do When Blocked

If Claude is asked to write Fit For Gov content and cannot identify a narrative strand that fits the topic, the correct move is to pause and flag the question to the user rather than inventing a strand. New strands enter the system deliberately; they are not improvised inside individual posts. The existing strands cover most of the threat-landscape content Fit For Gov publishes against, and most "there's no strand for this" situations on inspection turn out to be "I haven't re-read the strands file recently."
