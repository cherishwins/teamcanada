# Nuclear weapons accidents, 1945 to present: research file

Research base for a public paper. Not the paper. Compiled 1 November 2026.

## How to read this file

- Scope: accidents involving nuclear WEAPONS (bombs, warheads, weapon components, weapon-carrying platforms with weapons aboard). Not reactor accidents, not deliberate tests.
- Every row in the main tables cites a page that was actually opened and read for this file. Search-result snippets alone were never used as a source.
- Where a source states something, the wording here follows the source. Where sources disagree, both are given and the disagreement is named.
- Yields of US weapons are classified. Every yield below is a published estimate, and the source of each estimate is named.
- Anything that could not be pinned down is in section 6, not in the tables.

## 1. Main catalogue

"HE" means the conventional high explosive in the weapon. "Capsule" means the separate fissile core used in early designs. Unless a row says otherwise, no nuclear yield occurred: DoD 1981 states that "There never has been even a partial inadvertant [sic] U.S. nuclear detonation" and that "All 'detonations' reported in the summaries involved conventional high explosives (HE) only."

### 1c. United Kingdom

## 3. Counts and their definitions

### 3a. The DoD's own count: 32 "accidents", 1950–1980

- **Source:** DoD 1981. "Attached are unclassified summaries describing the circumstances surrounding 32 accidents involving nuclear weapons."
- **The 1981 document never uses the phrase "Broken Arrow".** It says "accident". Schlosser (2023) describes "Broken Arrow" as the military's flag word for the same category. Do not write "the DoD counted 32 Broken Arrows" as a quotation.
- **The definition, as printed in DoD 1981:** "An unexpected event involving nuclear weapons or nuclear weapons components that results in any of the following:
  - Accidental or unauthorized launching, firing, or use, by U.S. Forces or supported allied forces, of a nuclear-capable weapon system which could create the risk of an outbreak of war.
  - Nuclear detonation.
  - Non-nuclear detonation or burning of a nuclear weapon or radioactive weapon component, including a fully assembled nuclear weapon, an unassembled nuclear weapon, or a radioactive nuclear weapon component.
  - Radioactive contamination.
  - Seizure, theft, or loss of a nuclear weapon or radioactive nuclear weapon component, including jettisoning.
  - Public hazard, actual or implied."
- **Method:** "compiled by DOD/DOE researchers during December 1980–January 1981... applying current definitions." "To the best of our knowledge, this list is complete." DoD also says it is "possible but not likely" that an early accident went unreported.
- **Concealment built into the list:** "due to diplomatic considerations, it is not possible to specify the location of the accidents that occurred overseas, except for Palomares and Thule." That is why the Quebec drop appears as "Over Water, outside United States".
- **Sandia's breakdown (Brodie 1987):**
  - "None of the 32 accidents produced any measurable nuclear yield and only 5 resulted in radioactive contamination beyond the immediate accident site."
  - "Of the 32 accidents, 29 have been with weapons in Air Force custody."
  - If accidents involving only components, or weapons without sealed pits, were excluded, "the US would have counted only 19 nuclear weapon accidents."
- **DoD 1981 on contamination:** "Only two accidents, those at Palomares and Thule, resulted in a widespread dispersal of nuclear materials."
- **Army and Marine Corps:** DoD 1981 says the Army never had an event serious enough to be listed, and the Marine Corps has no peacetime custody of nuclear weapons.

### 3b. The count disputed: about 1,000 accidents and incidents, 1957–1967

- Eric Schlosser obtained by FOIA a 225-page DoD document, "Accidents and Incidents Involving Nuclear Weapons", apparently dated 15 October 1967. It lists "about 1,000 accidents and incidents, just from July 1957 to March 1967."
- Schlosser: "that 32 number is completely arbitrary... there have been hundreds of serious incidents, if not thousands."
- Source: [Bulletin interview, 2023 (NSArchive copy)](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/2023-05/2023-05-09_thebulletin.org-interview_with_eric_schlosser_why_we_cant_trust_the_governments_figures_about_nuclear_close_calls.pdf).
- **Why the two counts differ:** "accident" is a defined category; "incident" is wider (DoD 1981 already moves the Terrier event into a lower "significant incident" class). The two numbers measure different things and do not contradict each other. The paper should not set them side by side as if they did.

### 3c. US weapons never recovered

There is no single authoritative total. This count was made from the DoD 1981 text, event by event, and is not quoted from any source:

- **Whole weapons not recovered, per DoD's own text: 5.**
  - 1957 C-124 off the east coast: 2.
  - 1958 Tybee: 1.
  - 1959 P-5M in Puget Sound: 1 (an anti-submarine weapon with no nuclear material).
  - 1965 A-4E: 1.
- **Plus two capsules** (the 1956 Mediterranean B-47).
- **Plus part of a weapon** (Goldsboro: "A portion of one weapon, containing uranium").
- **Plus the classified "Spring 1968" entry.** If that is Scorpion, as the IAEA listing implies, add 2 nuclear torpedoes, for 7 whole weapons.
- **Thule's missing secondary** is not mentioned in DoD 1981. It comes from Christensen's DIIS study, as reported by NSArchive.
- Popular totals ("six lost bombs" and similar) were not verified and should not be used without a source.

### 3d. IAEA count of losses at sea, worldwide

IAEA-TECDOC-1242 (2001): "There are seven recorded accidents listed in Appendix I that have resulted in the confirmed loss of one or more nuclear weapons." This is a marine-environment inventory, so it leaves out land accidents. It covers both the US and the Soviet Union.

### 3e. United Kingdom

- **2001 MoD table:** 7 "accidents" since 1966 plus 12 incidents from the Oxburgh report, for 19 rows.
- **Oxburgh report (1992), unclassified summary:** "some twenty" accidents and incidents since 1960 (as quoted by Nukewatch).
- **SGR / NIS (2018):** the official list deposited in 2003 "detailed 27 incidents" and is "far from being a complete list".
- **NIS, *Playing with Fire* (2017):** "110 serious incidents" (as summarised by SGR; the report itself was not read).
- **Why these disagree:** different start dates, and different thresholds for "accident" versus "incident".

## 4. Disclosure asymmetry

The US list is the longest mainly because the US published one: DoD 1981, after an Air Force release of 26 summaries in 1977 and a 1968 "Chronology of Nuclear Accident Statements".

That openness was real but partial:
- DoD 1981 withheld every overseas location except Palomares and Thule.
- It put the 1965 Ticonderoga loss "more than 500 miles from land". In 1989 a US official placed it about 80 miles from the Okinawa island chain.
- It left at least one serious overseas event off the list entirely (Lakenheath, January 1961, revealed by FOIA in 2013).

The UK has published one official table (2001, deposited 2003) and an unclassified Oxburgh summary. Its earlier position, given to Parliament in July 1982 after HMS Sheffield sank, was that "there has never been any incident involving a British nuclear weapon leading to its loss or to the dispersal of radioactive contamination" (quoted by the IAEA). SGR/NIS write that "There is no central record of incidents, and records prior to 1960 are particularly patchy."

France has published no list. It confirmed the 2009 collision only after it appeared in the British press.

The Soviet Union disclosed some events at the time and others only later:
- **K-219 (1986):** the Politburo itself decided to inform the US, the IAEA and TASS.
- **Komsomolets (1989):** monitored jointly with Norway since 1990.
- **Others:** the IAEA's Soviet entries marked "Accident not confirmed" show how much is still unacknowledged.

China has disclosed nothing found here.

The paper should therefore treat the length of the US list as a measure of disclosure, not of how careless the US was. The countries with the shortest lists are the ones that have published least.

### 5b. Detail behind the verdicts

**Claim 2 (Goldsboro bomb 1).**
- Bomb 1 (aft bay) parachuted and landed "intact with only minor damage" (Sandia 1961).
- The safing pins were pulled by lanyards during the breakup, and the fuzing sequence began on both bombs (Sandia 1961).
- Brodie 1987: "The nonoperation of the cockpit-controlled ready-safe switch prevented nuclear detonation of this bomb."
- Precisely, the switch that held was the MC-772 Arm/Safe switch inside the bomb, controlled from the cockpit through the T-249 box.
- Jones's own count of the safeguards: the bomb had four safety devices, not six. "One 'set off' by the fall. Two rendered ineffective by aircraft breakup. That's right - ONE!"
- Which bomb was closer: NSArchive first said bomb 2 and corrected itself to bomb 1.

**Claim 3 (Goldsboro bomb 2).**
- Sandia 1961: bomb 2's "parachutes... did not deploy".
- DoD 1981: "A portion of one weapon, containing uranium, could not be recovered despite excavation... to a depth of 50 feet. The Air Force subsequently purchased an easement requiring permission for anyone to dig there."
- Brodie 1987: "One of the secondary subassemblies was not recovered."
- National Archives blog: "The 'secondary,' however, was never found."
- Depth is disputed: DoD gives 50 ft of excavation; the National Archives blog says the bomb buried itself "more than 180 feet deep".

**Claim 5 (Mars Bluff).**
- No capsule: "No capsule of nuclear materials was aboard the B-47 or installed in the weapon" (DoD).
- Military Times says accounts differ on whether the core was aboard. The DoD text is the authority here.
- House: "torn to shreds" (Smithsonian); "shredded" (Military Times).
- Injuries, no deaths: confirmed by DoD, Smithsonian and Military Times.
- **Crater:** the only fetched source with a figure, Post and Courier via Military Times, gives "some 24 feet deep and 50 feet wide" (about 15 m). Other sources, seen in search results but not read, give 70–75 ft (21–23 m). Write "about 15 to 20 m (50 to 70 ft); sources differ."

**Claim 6 (Damascus).**
- Warhead type W53: Encyclopedia of Arkansas.
- Yield 9 Mt: Nuclear Weapon Archive. The Encyclopedia gives no yield; the official yield is classified.
- Thrown from the silo: the warhead "landed about 100 feet from the launch complex's entry gate".
- No detonation and no leak: the safety features "prevented any loss of radioactive material" (Encyclopedia of Arkansas); "There was no radioactive contamination" (DoD).

**Claim 9 (Walske criteria).** Drell 1990, pp. 12–13, quoting a 14 March 1968 letter and an April 1968 memo from Carl Walske, then chairman of the DoD Military Liaison Committee.

| Criterion | What the claim said | What the source says |
|---|---|---|
| Normal environments | <1 in a billion lifetime | Premature nuclear detonation probability "shall not exceed... 1 in 10^9 per bomb (warhead) lifetime", prior to receipt of the prearm signal. |
| Abnormal environments | <1 in a million per weapon per abnormal environment | "1 in 10^6 per warhead exposure or accident". The claim's "per abnormal environment" should read "per exposure or accident". |
| One-point safety | <1 in a million chance of >4 lb TNT | "In the event of a detonation initiated at any one point in the high explosive system, the probability of achieving a nuclear yield greater than four (4) pounds TNT equivalent shall not exceed one in one million." It also says one-point safety "shall be inherent in the nuclear design". |

**Claim 10 (Drell panel on the W88).**
- The panel wrote that "the design choices that were made for the W88 in 1983 raise safety questions: the warheads are not equipped with IHE and are mounted in a through-deck configuration in close proximity to the third-stage rocket motor that uses a high energy 1.1 class detonable propellant."
- The panel's concern was that the third-stage motor detonating in a loading accident could set off the warhead HE, "leading to dispersal of plutonium, or even the initiation of a nuclear yield beyond the four-pound criterion".

**Claim 13 (W76-2).**
- The current FAS estimate is 8 kt: "a single-stage low-yield modification of the W76-1 with an estimated yield of 8 kilotons" (FAS 2025).
- 5–7 kt is the Nuclear Weapon Archive figure.
- The official yield is unpublished. Use "about 8 kt (FAS estimate)" or give the range with both sources.

**Claim 15 (pit production).**
- DOE: "in 1989, Rocky Flats was raided by the FBI/EPA... and all nuclear operations were suspended... Weapons component production was never to resume." The weapons production mission was declared officially over in 1993.
- Drell 1990 says W88 production was "interrupted... by the shutdown of the Rocky Flats plant where new pits... are manufactured".
- Production later restarted on a small scale at Los Alamos. FAS 2025 reports the first production pit for the W87-1 in October 2024.
- Write "large-scale pit production stopped in 1989", not "the US stopped making pits".

## 6. Unverified, disputed, or out of scope

### Unverified (do not use as fact)

1. **"Five of six" (or "six of seven") Goldsboro safety steps failed.** This originated with Ralph Lapp's *Kill and Overkill*, quoted in the Jones memo, which also called the weapon "24 megaton". Jones rebutted both ("Not six. The bomb had four"). The National Archives blog (2021) and the NSArchive's Unredacted blog (2013, "two 24-megaton weapons") repeat versions of these figures. **Do not use.** Schlosser (2023 caption) gives "Three of the four switches... had already triggered", which is consistent with Jones.
2. **Goldsboro bomb 2's HE.** Brodie 1987 says the free-falling bomb "experienced HE detonation upon impact". Sandia 1961 says "no HE explosion resulted" and DoD 1981 says "No explosion occurred." The two contemporaneous sources agree there was no HE detonation. Flag as disputed. Do not state that bomb 2's HE exploded.
3. **Goldsboro date.** NSArchive EBB 475's photo caption says "February 1961"; Sandia 1961 and DoD 1981 say 24 January 1961. Use 24 January.
4. **1950 B-36 off British Columbia.**
   - **Drop location.** DoD says "Pacific Ocean, off Coast of British Columbia". Brodie says "jettisoned in Puget Sound". The IAEA says "off Puget Sound".
   - **Wreck location.** DoD says the wreck "was later found on Vancouver Island". History.com says Mount Kologet in inland BC, found about three years later and destroyed by a demolition crew in 1954.
   - **Possible 2016 find.** A diver reported a possible bomb off Pitt Island near Haida Gwaii, and DND said it was of "keen interest" (CBS, 2016). This was not resolved in anything read here.
5. **1950 Quebec drop: date and weight.** The Globe/CP (2000) gives "January 10, 1950" and a 2,000-kg bomb. DoD 1981 and Canada's History give 10 November 1950, and Canada's History says 10,000 lb. Use 10 November 1950. The weight is unverified. A figure of about 100 lb of scattered uranium circulates but was not verified.
6. **18 Mar 1953 B-36 "off Newfoundland" (Canada).** The IAEA lists "Nuclear material" with the footnote "The US Government has neither confirmed nor denied the presence of nuclear material." It is not in DoD 1981. Unconfirmed. This is a possible second Atlantic Canadian case.
7. **IAEA US-table oddities.** "10 Mar 1956 B-47, Red Sea" (DoD says Mediterranean) and "5 Mar 1958 B-47, off Georgia" (Tybee was 5 Feb). Treat the IAEA's US date and place entries as less reliable than DoD's.
8. **Tybee capsule.** Claims that a 1966 congressional letter called the Tybee bomb a "complete weapon" with capsule were not found in any source read. The Air Force position (no capsule) stands in DoD 1981 and AP/CBS 2005.
9. **Ticonderoga location.** DoD 1981: "more than 500 miles from land". AP 1989: "about 80 miles from the closest point of the Okinawa island chain" and also "about 200 miles from Okinawa Island". IAEA: "250 miles South of Kyushu, 70 miles east of Okinawa", 27°35'N 131°19'E. The B43 designation was not verified in any source read here (only "one-megaton").
10. **Thule.** The claim that a whole bomb is missing (BBC, 2008) is rejected by Christensen's DIIS study, as summarised by NSArchive. The unrecovered secondary ("spark plug", "marshal's baton") is supported by NSArchive's summary of DIIS. The DIIS report itself (ft.dk) refused fetches.
11. **Lakenheath, 27 Jul 1956.** DoD's "Overseas Base" B-47-into-igloo entry is often identified as RAF Lakenheath. No source read here makes that identification.
12. **Sidi Slimane, 31 Jan 1958.** DoD's "Overseas Base" B-47 fire is probably the "recent nuclear mishap at the U.S. Air base in Sidi Slimane, Morocco" mentioned in a 12 March 1958 State Department letter (NSArchive EBB 475, doc 2). That link is an inference.
13. **Soviet events the IAEA marks "Accident not confirmed":**
    - 10 Jan 1970, Bay of Naples: a submarine with nuclear torpedoes.
    - Apr 1970, NE Atlantic: a submarine with 4 nuclear weapons.
    - Sep 1974, Black Sea: a Kashin-class destroyer with nuclear weapons.
    - Jun 1983, off Kamchatka: a submarine with 8 nuclear weapons.
14. **K-8 death toll and torpedo count.** Search results gave 52 dead and 4 nuclear torpedoes; neither was read in a primary source.
15. **K-129 torpedoes recovered?** NSArchive (citing *Blind Man's Bluff*) and the IAEA suggest the raised bow held nuclear torpedoes. The CIA history is silent. Unresolved.
16. **"Three officers had to agree" on B-59.** Not supported by NSArchive Briefing Book 808, which attributes the outcome to Arkhipov calming Savitsky.
17. **Norway 1995: whether one or several briefcases were activated.** One thesis says three. AHF says one briefcase, Yeltsin's.
18. **China 1993.** A declassified 1995 US intelligence digest, quoted by Jeffrey Lewis, said "a hydronuclear experiment went out of control at China's Southwest Institute of Fluid Physics in 1993, resulting in contamination". Lewis calls the surrounding analysis "conjecture and circumstantial evidence". Single-source; hydronuclear test, not a weapon accident. ([Arms Control Wonk, 2009](https://www.armscontrolwonk.com/archive/202239/subcritical-testing-at-lop-nor/))
19. **W88 Alt 370 and insensitive HE.** FAS 2025 says the life-extension replaces "the conventional high explosives with insensitive high explosives". This was not checked against DOE's Stockpile Stewardship plan. Verify before printing, because it bears directly on the Drell concern.
20. **Popular total of US weapons "lost".** Not verified. See the computed count in 3c.

### Out of scope, noted for completeness

- **1962 Thor test failures at Johnston Island (2 and 19 June 1962).** Missiles carrying nuclear test devices were destroyed in flight. Plutonium-contaminated debris was recovered from the lagoon after the second. These were test-programme accidents, listed by the IAEA ([IAEA 2001](https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/publications/PDF/te_1242_prn.pdf)), and fall under the "not deliberate tests" exclusion only loosely. Decide editorially.
- **Reactor accidents on weapon-carrying submarines** (Thresher 1963, K-19 1961, K-431 1985) are reactor accidents and excluded.
- **Windscale 1957** (a plutonium-production reactor fire) is a reactor accident and excluded, though SGR lists it in the UK weapons-programme history.
