# Bring the pensions home: the infrastructure side

Research file for the paper. Compiled 1 November 2026. Scope: Canadian
infrastructure that pension capital could own or finance, and how ownership
works here. Fund assets and holdings are out of scope; another file covers them.

Rules for this file: every figure carries its date or period and the page it
came from. Every link below was fetched while compiling this file. Where a page
could not be fetched, the claim is in "Unverified / disputed" at the end.

---

## 1. What the country owns, and what shape it is in

### 1.1 The newest edition: Canada's Core Public Infrastructure Survey, 2024 (released 30 October 2026)

Statistics Canada released the 2024 edition two days before this file was
compiled. It replaces the 2022 figures that most current commentary still uses.

Source: The Daily, "Canada's Core Public Infrastructure Survey, 2024", released
2026-10-30.
https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/261030/dq261030b-eng.htm

Exact quotes:

- "At the end of 2024, municipalities and other local and regional government
  organizations were responsible for over $2.0 trillion of core public
  infrastructure. The estimated replacement value of wastewater, potable water
  and stormwater assets accounted for more than half (52%) of the total value of
  core public infrastructure, while the value of roads, bridges and tunnels
  accounted for 30%."
- "This includes almost 2 million kilometres of linear assets and close to
  280,000 non-linear assets, with more than four-fifths of each being owned by
  local and regional governments."
- "While the condition of roads owned by municipalities improved slightly in
  2024 compared with 2022, water, wastewater and stormwater infrastructure
  assets deteriorated. Assets rated in poor or very poor condition used for
  potable water supply and for wastewater and stormwater management had an
  estimated replacement value of $124.3 billion in 2024, representing 14% of the
  total value of these assets and increasing 3 percentage points from 2022.
  Nonetheless, municipal roads had the highest estimated replacement value for
  assets rated in poor or very poor condition ($86.6 billion)."

Scope note, checked against the table: the $2.0 trillion and the 52% are for
local and regional government organizations, not all governments. The $124.3
billion and 14% are for municipally owned water assets. Recomputed from table
34-10-0284-01 (below): municipal potable water, wastewater and stormwater in
poor or very poor condition sum to $124.27 billion out of $863.4 billion
(14.4%); local and regional water assets total $1,049.4 billion out of $2,007.0
billion (52.3%).

Definitions, from the same release's note to readers:

- Replacement value: "the approximate cost, at the end of the reference year,
  that would be required to replace the assets, including demolition costs, but
  excluding land costs and overhead. These values differ from book value
  (accumulated cost) and measures of gross stock".
- Poor: "Failure likely and substantial work required in the short term. Asset
  barely serviceable. No immediate risk to health or safety."
- Very poor: "Immediate need to replace most or all of the asset. Health and
  safety hazards exist which present a possible risk to public safety ...
  Major work or replacement required urgently."
- "the term 'public' refers to assets that are owned or leased by provincial,
  territorial, regional and municipal governments."

### 1.4 The 2020 edition and the "60%" ownership figure

"Canada's Core Public Infrastructure Survey: Replacement values, 2020", The
Daily, 20 March 2023 (archived).
https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/230320/dq230320a-eng.htm

- "At the end of 2020, core public infrastructure in Canada, excluding social
  and affordable housing, had a total replacement value of $2.1 trillion."
- "water infrastructure ($771.8 billion) accounted for more than one-third
  (35.8%)."
- "Municipalities ($1,328.5 billion) owned 61.7% of the total estimated
  replacement value of core public infrastructure in Canada."
- "Wastewater infrastructure had the largest value of all infrastructure
  categories in urban municipalities at $205.7 billion (22.7% of the total)."
- Note: the same release says the poor or very poor share is "just over 10%"
  in its first paragraph and "12.3% of the total" later. Both are StatCan's
  words; $264.7 billion over $2.1 trillion is 12.3%. Quote the specific one.

The "municipalities own about 60%" claim, checked:

- 2016 Canadian Infrastructure Report Card, key messages: "Almost 60% of
  Canada's core public infrastructure is owned and maintained by municipal
  governments."
  https://canurb.org/wp-content/uploads/Canadian_Infrastructure_Report_Card_Key_Messages_2016-1.pdf
- StatCan 2020: municipalities 61.7% (above).
- StatCan 2022, computed from table 34-10-0284-01: municipalities 63.7% of
  replacement value across the nine asset classes; local and regional
  organizations (which includes regional governments and bodies that are not
  municipalities) 73.0%.
- For water specifically, the share is far higher: local and regional bodies
  held 95.9% of water replacement value in 2022 and 99.2% in 2020 (computed).

So "about 60%" is right for municipalities and all core infrastructure, and
low for water. Say "nearly two-thirds" for municipalities (2022), or "almost
all" for water.

### 2.2 Public-private partnership: Infrastructure Ontario, and what the auditor found

How the model works, from the Auditor General of Ontario's 2024 audit
"Procurement and Delivery of Selected Infrastructure Projects":
https://www.auditor.on.ca/en/content/annualreports/arreports/en24/pa_infrastructureON_en24.pdf

- A P3 "involves entering into one contract with a private-sector partner,
  often a consortium of several companies teaming up to work together to
  design, build, finance, operate and/or maintain the infrastructure asset."
- "The P3 model allows the Province to lock in a fixed price when the contract
  is signed ... for construction costs and the initial 30-year
  operating/maintenance period". "The Province also did not have to pay a
  substantial portion of the construction costs until the project was
  completed".
- Scale: "As of October 2024, Infrastructure Ontario (IO) had 30 infrastructure
  projects in pre-construction or construction phase with an estimated value of
  $58 billion, and an additional 11 projects in the procurement phase with an
  estimated construction value totalling $10 billion."
- 2024 findings: since 2021 "the Province has taken on greater risks and
  transferred fewer risks to the private sector"; requests for qualifications
  "went from averaging five interested parties in 2014 to averaging 2.5
  interested parties between 2020 and 2023"; "Of the three hospital projects
  procured under the new Progressive P3 model ... two projects still received a
  single bid"; Trillium Health Partners Mississauga Hospital's estimated costs
  "exceed the budget by $4 billion" (October 2024); Lakeridge Gardens and
  Highway 427 were over budget "by 27% and 15%, respectively".

The 2014 finding, quoted exactly. News release, 9 December 2014:
https://www.auditor.on.ca/en/content/news/14_newsreleases/2014news_3.05.pdf
Chapter 3.05: https://www.auditor.on.ca/en/content/annualreports/arreports/en14/305en14.pdf

- "For 74 projects that were either completed or under way under
  Infrastructure Ontario, tangible costs, such as construction, finance and
  professional services, were estimated to be nearly $8 billion higher under
  the Alternative Financing and Procurement (AFP) approach than they were
  estimated to have been if the projects had been delivered by the public
  sector ... About $6.5 billion of this is due to higher private-sector
  financing costs."
- "Infrastructure Ontario estimated that this $8-billion difference was more
  than offset by the risk of potential cost overruns if the construction and,
  in some cases, maintenance of these 74 facilities was done by the public
  sector."
- "There was no empirical data to support the key assumptions Infrastructure
  Ontario uses to assign costs to specific risks". Two risks "should not have
  been included; without them, public-sector delivery for 18 of these projects
  would have been assessed as $350 million cheaper than delivery under AFP."
- "As at March 31, 2014, there were almost $23.5 billion in liabilities and
  commitments that current and future governments will have to pay for AFP
  projects."

How to state it accurately: the $8 billion is the gap in estimated tangible
costs before any value is assigned to risk transfer, and $6.5 billion of it
is the cost of private finance. It is not a measured overrun. The auditor's
point was that the risk values used to justify it had no empirical basis.

A water P3 Canadians voted for: Regina, 2013.

- Global News, 25 September 2013: unofficial result "No" 57.1%, "Yes" 42.9%,
  just over 49,000 ballots. The "No" side was the P3 option. Federal
  commitment $58.5 million.
  https://globalnews.ca/news/864292/voters-choose-p3-funding-model
- Water Canada, 15 July 2014: Regina "finalized an agreement with EPCOR Water
  Prairies Inc. to design, build, finance, operate, and maintain its new sewage
  treatment plant for 30 years." Construction $181 million; 30-year cost $611
  million, "down from the $858 million originally projected"; "The P3 Canada
  Fund will provide up to 25 per cent of capital costs".
  https://www.watercanada.net/regina-finalizes-agreement-with-treatment-plant-contractor/

### 2.4 A pension fund that owns Canadian infrastructure: CDPQ and the REM

- Financing: CDPQ Infra $4.97B; Government of Québec $1.28B; Canada

## 3. Public opinion and history on private water

### 3.1 Polls (all commissioned by unions; no independent national poll found)

| Poll | Date | Sponsor | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| EKOS, 1,014 Canadians, ±3.1 | fieldwork 12-29 Jan 2001 | CUPE | "Fully three-quarters support public control of water services." | https://cupe.ca/ekos-poll-shows-canadians-reject-privatization |
| Ipsos Reid, 1,000 British Columbians | fieldwork 3-9 Oct 2005; released 31 Oct 2005 | CUPE | 75% opposed privatization of water services (60% strongly), 20% supported; 88% agreed water "should always remain in public hands"; 71% trust local government more for safe water, 21% a private company | https://www.ipsos.com/en-ca/british-columbians-keep-drinking-water-local-government-hands |

Against those: Regina's 2013 referendum, an actual vote, chose the P3 for its
wastewater plant 57.1% to 42.9% (2.2 above). Canadians defend public water;
they have also voted for private finance and operation of a public plant when
the price was shown to them.

### 3.2 Ontario, 2025-26: water corporations, publicly owned by law

Environmental Registry notice 025-1098:
https://ero.ontario.ca/notice/025-1098

- Bill 60, Fighting Delays, Building Faster Act, 2025, introduced 23 October
  2025, "received Royal Assent on November 27, 2025." It creates the Water and
  Wastewater Public Corporations Act, 2025, which "authorizes the Minister to
  require municipalities" to deliver water and wastewater through a new
  public corporation.
- First case: Peel. A corporation "jointly owned by Mississauga, Brampton and
  Caledon", transfer effective "January 1, 2029, or a different date as
  prescribed by the Minister"; the government committed to "keeping the
  proposed new corporation publicly-owned."

Peel Region's letter on the follow-up amendments (Bill 98, ERO 026-0301), 28
April 2026:
https://ero.ontario.ca/public/public_uploads/2026-04/Bill%2098%20ERO%20026-0301%20Amedments%20to%20WWPC%20Act%20and%20SDWA.pdf

- "Peel Region welcomes the requirement for 100 percent public sector ownership
  of water and wastewater public corporations set out in the proposed
  legislation". It also cites "the restriction on transferring municipal debt
  to a water and wastewater public corporation".

Whether Bill 98 has passed was not confirmed (see Unverified).

### 3.3 Hamilton-Wentworth, 1994-2004: an operating contract, not a sale

Source: Martin Pigeon, "Who takes the risks? Water remunicipalisation in
Hamilton, Canada", chapter 5 of Remunicipalisation: Putting Water Back into
Public Hands (Municipal Services Project / Transnational Institute, 2012). An
advocacy-leaning academic source; facts below are the dated ones.
https://www.queensu.ca/municipal-services-project/sites/mspwww/files/uploaded_files/Book%20PDF/Remunicipalisation/Chapter5_Hamilton.pdf

- City leaders "signed a 10-year, $180-million contract on December 30, 1994".
- The contractor, Philip Utilities Management Corporation, was "owned at 70%
  by Philip Environmental and 30% by the Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan
  Board." It "was signed without tender or public debate."
- Scope: operate and maintain "the water treatment facility, the three
  wastewater treatment plants and the 129 pumping stations", with "the rest of
  the infrastructure (distribution network and laboratories) falling under
  public management." The region kept ownership.
- "In April 1996 the workforce was cut in half, from 120 to 60. In January
  1996 the worst wastewater spill in the plant's history sent 180 million
  litres of raw sewage into the Hamilton harbour". The cleanup "costs were
  borne by the city".
- The contract passed to Azurix (an Enron subsidiary), then American Water
  Works. At renewal in 2004, American Water's compliant bid was "a yearly C$39
  million fee", against the city's expected C$12-17 million. On 13 September
  2004 the council committee voted 7 to 1 to bring operations back in-house.
- Afterwards the city's reports for 2005-2008 "document total savings of C$5.66
  million from the operating budget" (the author's summary of city reports).

For this paper: the first big Canadian water operating contract had a
Canadian pension fund as a 30% owner of the operator. Ownership of the assets
never left the public.

### 3.4 Walkerton, May 2000: a public utility, a privatized lab

What can be stated from fetched sources:

- City of Toronto staff report, 31 May 2002: "Over 2,000 people in the town
  became ill and seven died." "The Part 1 Report was made public on January
  18, 2002. The Commission's Part 2 Report ... was released to the public on
  May 23, 2002." O'Connor's Recommendation 44: "Municipalities should review
  the operating and management structure for their water system to ensure that
  it is capable of providing safe drinking water on a reliable basis." He sets
  out three options (municipal department, public utilities commission or
  municipal service board, municipally owned corporation) and "does not
  recommend one option over another."
  https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/2002/agendas/committees/pof/pof020611/it001.pdf
- CELA: "seven people died and over 2300 people became ill"; O'Connor made 121
  recommendations across both reports.
  https://cela.ca/safe-drinking-water-for-all-20-years-after-the-walkerton-inquiry-reports
- WaterWorld, 29 May 2002: Part One found the primary source of contamination
  was manure spread on a farm near Well 5.
  https://www.waterworld.com/drinking-water/article/16204448/part-two-of-walkerton-drinking-water-report-released
- O'Connor's Part Two on ownership, quoted in Eye Weekly (Brad MacKay and
  Jennifer Prittie, 6 June 2002, reposted by Environment Probe): "rarely does
  the discussion of privatization in the water industry in Ontario include the
  contention that ownership of water facilities and infrastructure should be
  placed in private hands" and "I am not convinced that either is uniquely able
  to operate a water system so as to achieve consistently high-quality
  drinking water."
  https://environment.probeinternational.org/2002/06/06/water-water-everywhere/

What Walkerton was not: it was not a private water system. The private piece
was laboratory testing, moved out of provincial labs in 1996 without a rule
that labs report bad results to health officials (see Unverified: the primary
report text could not be fetched; the Ontario archive returned an HTML page
instead of the PDF).

## 4. Inflation-linked and retail federal debt

### 4.1 Real Return Bonds ended, November 2022

Fall Economic Statement 2022, Annex 2 (page modified 2022-11-03):
https://budget.canada.ca/fes-eea/2022/report-rapport/anx2-en.html

- "The government has decided to cease issuance of Real Return Bonds (RRBs)
  effective immediately."
- "This decision reflects low demand for this product" and "will allow the
  government to promote liquidity by consolidating funding within our core
  funding sectors."

Reaction:

- Canadian Fixed-Income Forum (Bank of Canada advisory group of dealers and
  investors), meeting of 29 November 2022, minutes published 16 December 2022:
  members "disagreed with the government's reasons" and the committee
  "unanimously agreed" the minister should reconsider. Reported by Wealth
  Professional, 20 December 2022:
  https://www.wealthprofessional.ca/investments/fixed-income/feds-face-pushback-over-planned-end-to-inflation-protected-bonds/372469
- Canadian Institute of Actuaries, letter from president Hélène Pouliot to the
  finance minister (reported January 2023, first published in Benefits
  Canada): asked "that the decision to cease issuing real return bonds be
  reconsidered"; said "the current level of approximately $1 billion is not
  sufficient"; asked that "the $3.8 billion of real return bonds held by the
  Bank of Canada be sold into the market in an orderly fashion."
  https://legalwriter.net/2023/01/withdrawal-of-real-return-bonds-could-financially-impact-db-pension-plans-cia/
- C.D. Howe Institute, "Cancel the RRB Cancellation", William B.P. Robson and
  Alexandre Laurin, E-Brief, 22 August 2024. Surveyed 13 institutional
  investors (four jointly governed pension funds, five public investment
  managers, one insurer, three asset managers) holding "about $19 billion, or
  almost 40 percent of the stock of outstanding RRBs." None supported the
  decision (12 opposed, 1 no opinion); 8 disagreed that demand is low; 12 said
  liquidity is inadequate. Conclusion: "The federal government should resume
  issuing RRBs – in greater amounts and with more diversity of terms than
  before."
  https://cdhowe.org/publication/cancel-rrb-cancellation/

### 4.2 Canada Savings Bonds ended, 2017

Budget 2017, Annex 2 (22 March 2017):
https://www.budget.canada.ca/2017/docs/plan/anx-02-en.html

- "the Government of Canada will discontinue the sales of retail debt products
  in 2017." Reason: "Given an overall decline in the sales of Canada Savings
  Bonds products", competing consumer investments and administrative costs,
  "retail debt is no longer a cost-effective source of funds or a preferred
  investment by Canadians." Outstanding bonds honoured.

Investment Executive, 22 March 2017: budget documents attribute the decline to
"the proliferation of higher-yielding alternative retail investment
instruments"; holdings fell from more than $50 billion at the 1988-89 peak to
about $5 billion, less than 1% of federal market debt.
https://www.investmentexecutive.com/report-cards/special-reports/feds-eliminate-canada-savings-bonds-program/

The thread for the paper: in five years Ottawa closed both the retail bond and
the inflation-linked bond, each for low demand, and in April 2026 announced a
new retail product (section 5).

## 5. The Canada Strong Fund (announced 27 April 2026)

Department of Finance backgrounder, "Canada Strong Fund", 2026-04-27:
https://www.canada.ca/en/department-finance/news/2026/04/canada-strong-fund.html

Exact quotes:

- Size: "Initially, the government will provide $25 billion over 3 years, on a
  cash basis, to seed the Canada Strong Fund."
- Mandate: "the Fund will have a mandate to deliver market-rate returns for
  Canadians across the economy." It "will participate alongside other
  investors on a fully commercial basis."
- What it invests in: "From infrastructure to advanced manufacturing to energy
  and mining". The page also describes a focus primarily on equity, and
  working alongside the Canada Infrastructure Bank, Export Development Canada
  and the Business Development Bank of Canada (paraphrase from the fetch; get
  the exact sentence before quoting).
- Governance: "We will create a new Crown corporation" and "its work will be
  guided by a CEO and a qualified independent board of directors."
- Retail product: "any Canadian who wishes to can invest some of their savings
  into the Canada Strong Fund", "while their initial invested capital will be
  protected." Design is to be consulted on; no launch date or eligibility rule
  is given.
- Next step: "The government will establish a dedicated Canada Strong Fund
  Transition Office".
- The page does not mention pension funds.

Prime Minister's release, "Prime Minister Carney announces the Canada Strong
Fund – Canada's first sovereign wealth fund", 27 April 2026:
https://www.pm.gc.ca/en/node/52727

- "an initial federal contribution of $25 billion"; "a new arm's length entity
  reporting through the Minister of Finance and National Revenue"; a product
  "allowing individual Canadian investors to participate in Canada's growth";
  "This includes projects in clean and conventional energy, critical minerals,
  agriculture, and infrastructure."

Spring Economic Update 2026: Key Measures, 2026-04-28:
https://www.canada.ca/en/department-finance/news/2026/04/spring-economic-update-2026-key-measures.html

- "Introducing Canada's first national sovereign wealth fund, to invest in key,
  strategic Canadian projects and companies"; "Canadians will also have a
  stake in the projects themselves, allowing them to receive financial
  returns".

Status at 1 November 2026: no enabling bill, CEO, board, or retail launch date
was found. The Wikipedia article (last edited 18 October 2026) records nothing
after 27 April. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada_Strong_Fund

Note that the Canada Infrastructure Bank's 2026-27 plan already refers to
