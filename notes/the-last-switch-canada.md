# Canada's record of action around nuclear weapons: research notes

Compiled 1 November 2026 for a Northern Temper paper. Every fact in the main sections below comes from a page that was fetched during this research, and the link given is the page it came from. Quotations are exact as returned by the fetch, cut short where the tool would only return a fragment. Anything that turned up only in a search excerpt, or on a page that refused the fetch, is in the last section and not in the main notes. Accidents are deliberately out of scope; another file covers them.

A note on access: The Canadian Encyclopedia, NTI, CTBTO.org, ICAN, the UN Digital Library and the NORAD history office all refused fetches from this sandbox (403 or a blank shell). Their content appears here only where another source confirmed it.

## 1. The Manhattan Project, Chalk River, and the choice not to build

**The Government of Canada does say it, in so many words.** The Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission's fact sheet "Canada's historical role in developing nuclear weapons" (date modified 2025-11-25) states: "Canada was the first country with significant nuclear capability to reject nuclear weapons." https://www.cnsc-ccsn.gc.ca/eng/resources/fact-sheets/Canadas-contribution-to-nuclear-weapons-development

The same CNSC fact sheet also says:
- Canada "formally decided to enter the nuclear age" on 17 August 1942, and C.D. Howe "famously gave the go ahead for the Montreal Laboratory."
- During the war Canada "supplied and refined uranium for use in U.S. facilities," and Eldorado reopened a mine "to supply the U.S. military with the uranium needed to produce the nuclear bomb."
- Canada "continued to be a supplier of uranium for military purposes for two decades after the war," and "Between 1959 and 1964, about 252 kg of plutonium contained in used nuclear fuel was exported to the U.S." This is the counter-fact, and a paper that leaves it out could be checked against us on it.
- In 1965 the government decided that "all exports of uranium and all other nuclear materials would be for peaceful purposes only."
- After 1946, Chalk River "focused its efforts on medical and industrial applications of nuclear technology."
- It also says the 1965 policy "was reinforced in 1970 when Canada signed" the NPT. **That is wrong on the date:** Canada signed in 1968 and ratified in 1969 (see section 4). 1970 is the year the treaty entered into force.

**Parliament said it first, in a longer form.** The House of Commons Standing Committee on Foreign Affairs and International Trade (chair Bill Graham), report "Canada and the Nuclear Challenge: Reducing the Political Value of Nuclear Weapons for the Twenty-First Century", December 1998, 36th Parliament, 1st Session: "Canada participated in the development of the first atomic bomb and accepted nuclear weapons for its military forces during the Cold War; Canada also gained a unique voice on these issues as the first country capable of developing nuclear weapons to refuse to do so", and it continues "as the first to decide to divest itself of such weapons, and as a leader in the 1995 extension" of the NPT. https://www.ourcommons.ca/DocumentViewer/en/36-1/FAIT/report-7 (title and chair) and https://ourcommons.ca/documentviewer/en/36-1/FAIT/report-7/page-30 (quotation)

Recommended wording for the paper: quote the CNSC line and name the CNSC as its source. "Significant nuclear capability" is the government's own qualifier, so keep it. The 1998 committee wording ("capable of developing nuclear weapons") is stronger, and it is Parliament's wording, not the government's.

**Montreal Laboratory.** The Atomic Heritage Foundation / National Museum of Nuclear Science and History says it was "established in late 1942 by the National Research Council of Canada." Its first director was Hans Halban, and in April 1944 General Groves insisted that John Cockcroft replace him. At the Quebec meeting of 17 August 1943, Roosevelt and Churchill agreed arrangements "to ensure full and effective collaboration between the two countries in bringing the project to fruition." https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/location/canada

**ZEEP, 5 September 1945.** Canadian Nuclear Laboratories says ZEEP "first went critical on Sept. 5, 1945" and was "the first controlled nuclear chain reaction constructed outside of the United States." Its maximum heat output was 30 W. https://www.cnl.ca/?p=12773 The AHF gives 3:45 pm on 5 September 1945, calls it "the first operational nuclear reactor outside the United States", and says it was used for basic research until 1970. https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/location/canada The CNSC history page says "Canada's first nuclear reactor was ZEEP (zero energy experimental pile)," which "began operations at Chalk River in September 1945." https://www.cnsc-ccsn.gc.ca/eng/about-us/history/ Note the wattage: the CNL page says 30 W, and a secondary source in the search results said 10 W. Use CNL's figure, or leave the wattage out.

**Uranium: Port Radium and Port Hope.** The AHF says Gilbert Labine found radium and uranium at Port Radium, NWT, in May 1930. The mine reopened in 1942 to supply uranium for the bomb, and its ore was refined at Port Hope "before being delivered to Los Alamos." https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/location/canada (The nationalization date is in the unverified section.)

## 2. What Canada built instead

**NRX, 1947.** CNL: "NRX began operation on July 22, 1947," and it was "Canada's first large-scale research reactor." It "permanently ceased operation on April 8, 1993." https://www.cnl.ca/?p=17127 (Some secondary sources give a 30 March 1993 shutdown. Use CNL's date.)

**NRU, 1957 to 2018, and medical isotopes.** CNL's health-science timeline says NRU "goes online" in 1957. In 1970 came the "First Molybdenum-99 produced in NRU." Its 1974 entry reads: "Steadily delivering 30-40% of worldwide demand, at its peak as much as 80%." In 1949, "CRL begins to supply Cobalt-60 to industry." https://www.cnl.ca/health-science-2/timeline/
- The Government of Canada, responding to the Commons Natural Resources committee's report on the NRU shutdown (the committee reported 24 November 2010; the response cites January 2011 announcements), described "the NRU reactor providing approximately 30-40 percent of that supply prior to the outage." It also said Tc-99m "accounts for approximately 80 percent of nuclear medicine diagnostic procedures." https://www.ourcommons.ca/documentviewer/en/40-3/RNNR/report-2/response-8512-403-138
- World Nuclear News, 3 April 2018: NRU produced "about 40% of world supply of Mo-99." It stopped routine Mo-99 production in October 2016, and CNL said it was "quietly shut down for the last time" at 7:00 pm on 31 March 2018. https://world-nuclear-news.org/Articles/Canadian-isotope-reactor-enters-retirement
- Safe wording: "for decades, 30 to 40 percent of the world's molybdenum-99, and at its peak as much as 80 percent (CNL)". Do not print a single share without its years: the figure moved with other reactors' outages.

**CANDU.** CNL says the Nuclear Power Demonstration reactor at Rolphton, Ontario, came online in 1962 and was "the first nuclear power-generating reactor in Canada to prove the CANDU® concept." Later CANDU units came online in Ontario, Quebec and New Brunswick, and in Argentina, China, India, South Korea and Romania. https://www.cnl.ca/cnl_journey/nuclear-power-to-the-grid/ (The exact June 1962 grid date was not on the fetched page. CNL's list leaves out Pakistan, where the Canadian-supplied KANUPP unit was built; see the unverified section.)

**Cobalt-60 cancer therapy, autumn 1951. Both cities did it within days of each other, and each was first at something different:**
- **London, Ontario: first patient, 27 October 1951.** Western News, "Battista: Cobalt-60 celebrates 60 years" (Jason Winders, 3 November 2011): the world's first cobalt-60 cancer treatment took place on 27 October 1951 at Victoria Hospital (now the South Street site of London Health Sciences Centre). The source was installed on 16 October 1951. Clinical direction came from Ivan Smith, director of the Cancer Clinic at Victoria Hospital. The unit, the Eldorado "Cobalt Bomb", was developed by Eldorado Mining and Atomic Energy of Canada Limited. https://news.westernu.ca/?p=9157
- **Saskatoon: first patient treated with a calibrated dose, 8 November 1951.** The University of Saskatchewan timeline lists the "World's first calibrated Cobalt-60 cancer therapy unit" with the "world's first patient treated on 8 November 1951," after 11 weeks of calibration. https://cobalt60.usask.ca/timeline.php The USask exhibit names Dr. Harold Johns ("Canada's first full-time cancer physicist") as the designer and machinist John MacKay (Acme Machine and Electric Co.) as the builder. Graduate students Sylvia Fedoruk, Edward Epp, Douglas Cormack and Lloyd Bates helped. It says the two units were "completed and working within days of each other," that Saskatoon was first to treat a patient with an accurately calibrated dose, and that the unit treated 6,728 patients before it was replaced in 1972. https://cobalt60.usask.ca/exhibit.php The first patient was a 43-year-old mother of four with cervical cancer, who lived another 47 years. https://news.usask.ca/articles/general/2017/time-travel-october-1951.php
- **How many patients worldwide: there is no single checkable figure.** Western (2011): "approximately 35 million cancer patients worldwide have benefitted." USask (2017): by the end of the century cobalt-60 therapy was estimated to have helped "more than 70 million people," with no source given. The USask exhibit: "millions of cancer patients around the world." Neither number cites a method, and they disagree by a factor of two. On a checkable site the safe wording is "millions" (USask). Otherwise print both, each with its source.

## 3. US nuclear weapons in Canada, 1963 to 1984

- **BOMARC, North Bay and La Macaza.** Legion Magazine (Sharon Adams, 25 September 2019): "On Dec. 31, 1963, 56 BOMARC missiles with nuclear warheads were delivered to RCAF stations in North Bay, Ont." and La Macaza, Que. "The warheads remained U.S. property." Out of service by 1972. https://legionmagazine.com/bomarc-missiles-come-to-canada/ The RCAF's own history (On Windswept Heights II) places the missiles at North Bay and La Macaza "from 1961 to 1972" and says "The warheads arrived in 1963." https://www.canada.ca/en/air-force/corporate/reports-publications/on-windswept-heights-2/48-history-1950-1964.html The squadrons stood up before the warheads arrived; 31 December 1963 is the date the weapons arrived.
- **Honest John (Army, West Germany).** Friends of the Canadian War Museum, Fact Sheet No. 51 (Capt (N) (Ret'd) M. Braham): the Diefenbaker government approved the purchase in 1962. Two Surface-to-Surface Missile batteries were formed in September 1960. In December 1961 more than 225 men of 1st SSM Battery deployed to Hemer under 4 CIBG. Canadians in Europe used a 2 kt warhead; the battery had "a reported 16 of 69 available warheads" held by a US Army custodial detachment. 2nd SSM Battery disbanded in September 1968, and 1st SSM Battery "In 1970... was also disbanded." https://friends-amis.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/FS51_HonestJohn.pdf (The same sheet names the four nuclear-capable systems: BOMARC, Genie on the CF-101 Voodoo, Honest John, and bombs on the CF-104.)
- **CF-104 Starfighter (1 Air Division, Europe).** The RCAF's historical aircraft page: "The aircraft was capable of the nuclear strike role," and "Later in its Canadian Armed Forces career, the aircraft was returned to a conventional strike role." In service 1961 to 1984; no date given for the change of role. https://www.canada.ca/en/air-force/services/aircraft/canadair-starfighter.html The Air Force Museum of Alberta: select 1 Air Division pilots converted "from the conventional role of the Sabre fighter to the nuclear role of the CF-104 Starfighter." https://www.rcaf.museum/history-cold-war (The 1964 to 1972 dates are in the unverified section.)
- **Genie on CF-101 Voodoos, and the last weapons out.** The Montreal Gazette of 15 November 1984, reproduced at regimentalrogue.com: the "last nuclear weapons on Canadian soil were removed without fanfare in July [1984]," and "an estimated 55 nuclear-tipped Genie missiles" went back to the US from CFB Bagotville, Comox and Chatham. The Department of National Defence confirmed it the day before. https://regimentalrogue.com/bl2/Canadas-Last-Nukes-1984.html
- **The government's own line on 1984.** CNSC history (modified 2025-11-05): "1984 – With all nuclear weapons removed from Canada, the country becomes nuclear weapon-free." https://www.cnsc-ccsn.gc.ca/eng/about-us/history/ (A CNSC feature article gives the month as January 1984; see the disputed section.)

## 4. Disarmament: things Canada did

- **NPT.** Global Affairs Canada (Minister Champagne, 5 March 2020): "Canada signed the NPT on July 23, 1968 and ratified on January 8, 1969." https://www.canada.ca/en/global-affairs/news/2020/03/minister-of-foreign-affairs-marks-50th-anniversary-of-treaty-on-the-non-proliferation-of-nuclear-weapons.html The GAC library catalogue (Canada Treaty Series 1970 No. 7) adds that Canada signed at London and Washington on 23 July 1968 and at Moscow on 29 July 1968, deposited its ratification on 8 January 1969, and that the treaty entered into force on 5 March 1970. https://gac.canadiana.ca/view/ooe.b1639857
- **The Shannon Mandate, 24 March 1995.** The primary document is CD/1299, "Report of Ambassador Gerald E. Shannon of Canada on consultations on the most appropriate arrangement to negotiate a treaty banning the production of fissile material for nuclear weapons or other nuclear explosive devices", Conference on Disarmament, 24 March 1995. The mandate directs an ad hoc committee "to negotiate a non-discriminatory, multilateral and internationally and effectively verifiable treaty banning the production of fissile material for nuclear weapons or other nuclear explosive devices." It is the compromise that let everyone join: the mandate "does not preclude any delegation from raising for consideration in the Ad Hoc Committee" past production or the management of stocks. https://www.nti.org/documents/92/cd1299.pdf GAC's own account: Shannon "brokered the first real agreement on a negotiation mandate," and it was used to launch an Ad Hoc Committee in 1998. https://www.international.gc.ca/world-monde/issues_development-enjeux_developpement/peace_security-paix_securite/nuclear_radiological-nucleaire_radiologique.aspx?lang=eng
- **High-Level FMCT Expert Preparatory Group, 2017–2018.** GAC describes "a 25-member High-Level FMCT Expert Preparatory Group" whose work was linked to consultative meetings in New York in March 2017 and February 2018. "At its final meeting in June 2018, the Preparatory Group succeeded in negotiating a robust consensus report." (GAC page above.) Arms Control Today (Alicia Sanders-Zakre, July 2018): it was chaired by Canadian Ambassador Heidi Hulan and "completed its work June 8" with consensus recommendations, and Hulan called the report "two inches from a negotiating text." https://www.armscontrol.org/act/2018-07/news-briefs/fissile-material-report-points-toward-treaty (The UN document is A/73/159, 13 July 2018, per the UN Digital Library listing; the record itself refused the fetch.)
- **CTBT.** Signed 24 September 1996, the day it opened for signature; ratification deposited 18 December 1998 (Arms Control Association table). https://www.armscontrol.org/act/2001-01/comprehensive-test-ban-treaty-signatories-ratifiers GAC's page says "Canada ratified the CTBT on December 3, 1998"; see the disputed section.
- **Test-ban monitoring stations in Canada: sixteen.** GAC: "Sixteen IMS stations and laboratories are located in Canada," of 337 worldwide (GAC page above, modified 2024-06-24). NRCan gives the breakdown: 1 primary seismic array, 2 primary three-component seismic stations, 6 auxiliary seismic stations, 1 infrasound array, 1 T-phase station, 4 radionuclide stations and 1 radionuclide laboratory, which adds up to 16. NRCan is the National Authority for the seismic, infrasound and hydroacoustic stations, and Health Canada's Radiation Protection Bureau runs the radionuclide side. https://can-ndc.nrcan.gc.ca/index-en.php (modified 2019-03-01)
- **Yellowknife Seismological Array.** NRCan: "established in 1962" under an agreement between Canada's Department of Mines and Technical Surveys and the UK Atomic Energy Authority, to study "the feasibility of teleseismic detection and identification of nuclear explosions." In August 1995 it was chosen as one of the primary components of the IMS. https://can-ndc.rncan.gc.ca/yka/index-en.php So Canada was working on detecting nuclear tests 34 years before the treaty was signed.
- **IPNDV.** GAC: "In December 2014, the United States government and the Nuclear Threat Initiative (NTI) think tank launched the IPNDV," and "Since 2017, Canada has provided $2.5 million dollars" through the Weapons Threat Reduction Program (GAC page above). Canada hosted the seventh plenary, which closed Phase II, in Ottawa on 3–5 December 2019: 89 representatives from 24 countries and the EU. Participants then toured Chalk River for demonstrations of "experimental techniques for verifying the presence or absence of weapons-grade nuclear materials." Phase II found that "multilateral verification of nuclear dismantlement is possible." https://www.ipndv.org/news/ipndv-phase-ii-concludes-in-ottawa/ (9 January 2020)
- **Also on GAC's page:** Canada "strongly supported the Nuclear Security Summit (NSS) process between 2010 and 2016," and co-hosts the "Friends of the CTBT" ministerial meeting with Japan, Australia and the Netherlands.

## 5. Current position: the TPNW, NATO, and New START

- **GAC's own wording on the TPNW.** From the Minister of Foreign Affairs' briefing book for the Senate Foreign Affairs committee (AEFA), 8 June 2023 (published 16 January 2024). Key messages: "Canada is strongly committed to working with partners to achieve a world free of nuclear weapons." "We are not a signatory to the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons," "which is incompatible with our NATO commitments." "we share common ground with Treaty supporters" "in our efforts to accelerate the very slow pace of global nuclear disarmament." Background: "The TPNW is inconsistent with Canada's membership in NATO," which "relies on nuclear deterrence as a core aspect of its collective security." "While Canada does not support the Treaty," "it shares the underlying concerns about the very slow progress of disarmament." https://international.canada.ca/en/global-affairs/corporate/transparency/briefing-documents/parliamentary-committee/2023-06-08-aefa
- **Observer status: no.** The Nuclear Weapons Ban Monitor (Norwegian People's Aid) says Canada "boycotted the negotiations" in 2017 and voted no on the mandate resolution (A/RES/71/258). It records no Canadian participation at the first (2022), second (2023) or third (2025) Meetings of States Parties, and Canada has voted against every UNGA resolution on the TPNW, including in 2025. It also records a House of Commons motion of December 2024, passed unanimously, that honoured the hibakusha and encouraged the government to enhance engagement with the TPNW. https://banmonitor.org/profiles/canada (an NGO monitor, not a government source; the government's own record of the motion has not been fetched.)
- **NPT Review Conference, 27 April 2026.** Canada's general-debate statement (Richard Arbeiter, GAC): "As a NATO Ally, Canada understands the security realities"; as a non-nuclear-weapon state it sees "the risk of deferral becoming the default"; it urges the conference to "secure CTBT entry into force"; and "This Conference will be judged by results." https://www.international.gc.ca/world-monde/issues_development-enjeux_developpement/peace_security-paix_securite/weapon-armes-control-documents/nuclear-review-26.aspx?lang=eng (The outcome of the conference was not researched.)
- **UNGA First Committee, 21 October 2025.** Canada "commemorated the 80th anniversaries of the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki" and said "Eighty years later, nuclear weapons remain an existential threat to all of humanity." It called on Russia to return to "the full implementation of the New START Treaty" and on both parties to "enter negotiations for a successor agreement as soon as possible," and it asked China to "demonstrate needed transparency regarding its nuclear arsenal and doctrine." https://www.international.gc.ca/world-monde/issues_development-enjeux_developpement/peace_security-paix_securite/weapon-armes-control-documents/2025-10-uw-an.aspx?lang=eng
- **New START: expired.** Associated Press via PBS NewsHour, 11 February 2026: the treaty expired on 5 February, leaving no caps on the two largest arsenals (it had limited each side to 1,550 warheads and 700 deployed missiles and bombers). Lavrov: "The moratorium declared by the president will remain as long as the U.S. doesn't exceed these limits." Trump wants China in any successor, and Beijing has refused. https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/russia-says-it-will-stick-to-new-starts-nuclear-arms-limits-as-long-as-the-u-s-does Arms Control Today (Liang and Kimball, Jan/Feb 2026) reports Putin's offer of 22 September 2025 to keep observing the central limits for a year if the US "acts in a similar spirit." https://www.armscontrol.org/node/15049 No Canadian government statement on the expiry itself was found. Nothing after February 2026 was found either, so check for a successor arrangement before publishing.
- **Context for the paper (GAC briefing for FAAE, 27 November 2025):** the US President had said he "instructed the US Department of War to resume testing U.S. nuclear weapons"; GAC noted "Russia rescinded its CTBT ratification in 2023" and that the US "last conducted a physical nuclear weapons test in 1992." https://international.canada.ca/en/global-affairs/corporate/transparency/briefing-documents/parliamentary-committee/2025-11-27-faae (The same briefing calls Canada "a signatory of the Comprehensive Nuclear Test Ban Treaty". Canada has ratified it, not only signed.)

## 6. NORAD and the North Warning System

- National Defence (22 June 2022): in 1958 Canada and the United States "formally established the world's only binational military command." The North Warning System is "a series of remote radar sites that provide surveillance and early warning across northern Canada and Alaska". It replaced the DEW Line, beginning in 1985. Modernization: "$38.6 billion dollars over the next twenty years." https://www.canada.ca/en/department-national-defence/news/2022/06/north-american-aerospace-defense-command-norad.html
- RCAF (29 September 2020, archived): "the 50 radars of the North Warning System," 47 of them in Canada ("11 long-range sites and 36 short-range sites"), running from the southern coast of Newfoundland and Labrador to the North Slope of Alaska. They "can detect airborne threats across North America's polar region" and feed the Canadian Air Defence Sector at North Bay. https://www.canada.ca/en/department-national-defence/maple-leaf/rcaf/2020/09/maintaining-the-north-warning-system.html
- Why over Canada: Canada's Cold War Museum (Diefenbunker) says DEW Line operators "scanned the skies for Soviet nuclear bombers making their way across the North Pole." https://fading-signal.diefenbunker.ca/en/fox-main/eyes-on-the-skies
- Suggested line: "The shortest way from Eurasia to North America runs over the pole and down across Canada, so since 1958 Canadians have stood half of the watch: 47 of the 50 North Warning radars are on Canadian soil."

## Unverified / disputed

**Seen only in search excerpts, or the page refused the fetch. Do not print without a fetched source:**
- C.D. Howe telling the House of Commons on 5 December 1945 that Canada had not made atomic bombs and had "no intention" of making them. This turned up only in secondary search excerpts. The Hansard text was not found. It would be the best primary source for "the decision", if someone can pull Hansard for December 1945.
- The nationalization of Eldorado: shares taken by the Crown under PC 535 of 27 January 1944, or control bought in 1943. The sources differ, and none was fetched.
- CF-104 nuclear strike role from 1964 to 1972, and Genie service from 1965 to 1984. These come from The Canadian Encyclopedia, which refused fetches. Both are widely repeated and probably right, but neither was fetched.
- CTBTO station profile: PS09 Yellowknife was "the first primary seismic station to be certified" (28 July 2000). This is from a search excerpt of ctbto.org (403 on fetch). A CTBTO press release titled "Canada first State Signatory to have signed facilities agreement" also exists but could not be read.
- NPD's first power to the Ontario grid on 4 June 1962 (secondary sources only; CNL's page gives just 1962).
- KANUPP (Pakistan) as a CANDU export. It is not on CNL's list, so it was not verified here.
- "In 1972 Canada became the first country to sign a comprehensive nuclear safeguards agreement with the IAEA" (CNSC history page). Canada's INFCIRC/164 agreement entered into force on 21 February 1972 (IAEA listing seen in search only). Whether it was first is doubtful: Finland's NPT safeguards agreement may have entered into force earlier in February 1972. Not checked, so do not use "first".
- Roy Errington and Donald Green as the Eldorado engineers behind the London unit: commonly cited, but not on any fetched page.
- Bagotville museum: the last warheads left in April 1984 (the page answered 405 to the fetch).

**Disputed or wrong where commonly claimed:**
- **"Canada hosted US nuclear weapons in West Germany until 1984"** (Legion Magazine, 2019, and repeated elsewhere). This conflates two things. The Honest John battery in Germany disbanded in 1970 (Friends of the CWM), and the CF-104 went back to a conventional role well before it retired (RCAF; secondary sources put the change in 1972). What ended in 1984 was the Genie stockpile at the three bases in Canada.
- **The month in 1984.** A CNSC feature article (seen in search; its URL now 404s) says "In January 1984, Canada became nuclear-weapon free." The 1984 Gazette report, citing DND, says July 1984. The Bagotville museum reportedly says April. Print the year alone: "by 1984" or "in 1984".
- **NPT dates on Government of Canada pages disagree with each other.** GAC and the treaty record say signed 23 July 1968 and ratified 8 January 1969. The CNSC fact sheet says Canada "signed" in 1970, and the CNSC history page lists the ratification under 1958. Both CNSC dates are wrong. Cite GAC.
- **CTBT ratification date.** GAC's page says 3 December 1998; the deposit date in the treaty tables is 18 December 1998. The earlier date may be when the instrument was signed in Ottawa, but no source says so. Use "ratified in December 1998" or cite the deposit date.
- **BOMARC "from 1961".** The squadrons and missiles were there from 1961–62, but the nuclear warheads arrived on 31 December 1963. The RCAF history says the missiles were "first conventionally armed"; other sources describe them as unarmed until the warheads arrived. Not resolved, so say "nuclear-armed from 31 December 1963 to 1972."
- **"World's first cobalt-60 treatment" is claimed by both cities.** London treated the first patient (27 October 1951); Saskatoon claims the first calibrated treatment (8 November 1951). Present them as two Canadian teams, a few days apart.
- **Cobalt patients worldwide:** 35 million (Western, 2011) against more than 70 million (USask, 2017). Neither gives a method.
- **NRU's share of world Mo-99:** 30–40% (CNL, Government of Canada), "about 40%" (WNN), "up to 80%" at peak (CNL), and about 60% in a CNSC environmental-assessment notice (search only), which was during another reactor's outage. Give a range and its years, never one number.
- **ZEEP's power:** 30 W (CNL) against 10 W (secondary). Use CNL's figure or leave it out.
- **The "first country" claim is real, but qualified.** The government says "first country with significant nuclear capability to reject nuclear weapons" (CNSC). "The first country able to build the bomb that chose not to" is a paraphrase; quote the CNSC wording instead. The CNSC fact sheet also records the plutonium-bearing used fuel exported to the US from 1959 to 1964 and two decades of military uranium sales, and an honest paper names both alongside the claim.
- One national statement in the UN First Committee transcript for the 81st session (2026) reportedly named Canada among countries hosting US nuclear weapons (search excerpt only). If someone repeats this, it is false: none have been in Canada since 1984 (CNSC).
